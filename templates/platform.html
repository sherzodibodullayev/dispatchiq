The platform

Six pieces, one dispatch desk, and a line the AI does not cross

Everything on this page is the planned product described in the five-year business plan. None of it is shipped yet. Where the plan marks something as roadmap rather than initial product, this page says so on the section itself rather than in a footnote.

Section 7.1

Dispatcher dashboard

One consolidated view instead of a load board, a spreadsheet, a phone and an inbox. The dashboard is planned to show trucks, drivers, current assignments, available loads, pickup appointments, delivery appointments, operational alerts and documentation status on a single screen.

The point is not that any one of those is hard to find today. It is that finding all eight takes a dispatcher four applications and a memory of where things were left.

On the screen
  • Trucks and their current status
  • Drivers and availability
  • Current assignments
  • Available loads, ranked
  • Pickup and delivery appointments
  • Operational alerts and exceptions
  • Documentation status per load

AI dispatch copilot

Plain-language questions against live operational data, so the answer does not require navigating to wherever it happens to live.

Section 7.2 · Examples from the plan
  • “Which trucks are available tomorrow?”
  • “Show me loads that could fit Truck 14.”
  • “Which deliveries are at risk of being late?”
  • “Summarize today's open dispatches.”
  • “Prepare a message to the broker about the delay.”
What it is not

It is not a general-purpose chatbot pointed at a fleet, and it is not a booking agent. The last example on that list ends at prepare. A dispatcher reads the draft and decides whether it goes.

The same assistant answers questions on this website — the one in the corner. It is working from the business plan rather than from a live fleet, and it will tell you so.

Section 8

Load-matching engine

The engine evaluates truck location, destination, pickup time, delivery time, equipment type, cargo requirements, driver availability, estimated travel time and your own operational preferences, and produces a ranked list of loads that could work.

The ranking is a recommendation, not a decision. The dispatcher approves, modifies or rejects it. That is stated in the plan as a design requirement rather than a limitation of an early version — a machine that books freight on your authority is a different product with a different risk profile.

Factors weighed
  • Truck location and destination
  • Pickup and delivery times
  • Equipment type and cargo requirements
  • Driver availability
  • Estimated travel time
  • Operational preferences and lane history

Extracted information populates the dispatch workflow directly. Extraction accuracy is one of the things the Months 4–6 pilot program exists to measure — no accuracy figure is claimed here, because none has been measured yet.

Communication assistant

Communication is a major component of dispatch work, and most of it is the same five messages.

Brokers
  • Load inquiries
  • Rate requests
  • Appointment confirmations
  • Delay notifications
  • Status updates
Drivers
  • Dispatch instructions
  • Pickup information
  • Reminders
  • Appointment updates
Customers
  • Shipment status
  • Estimated arrival
  • Exception notifications
Sending is earned, not assumed. Automated sending is introduced gradually: human approval first, then predefined low-risk automated workflows once a customer has seen enough drafts to trust the pattern.
Section 4

Dispatch monitoring

Alerting on the things that are cheap to fix an hour early and expensive to discover an hour late.

Alerts
  • Approaching appointments
  • Delayed pickups
  • Delivery deadlines
  • Missing documentation
  • Changes to planned routes
  • Operational exceptions
Voice AI — roadmap, not product (Section 10). A future version is planned to let a dispatcher work hands-free: “Find available refrigerated loads for the Dallas truck,” answered out loud or on the dashboard. It is not built. Because voice creates additional operational and legal exposure, the plan introduces it progressively with consent, recording, privacy and audit controls where applicable. Anyone telling you it ships in the first release is guessing.

Where the human keeps authority

Section 5 — human-in-the-loop design.

The AI may
  • Recommend actions
  • Prepare communications
  • Organize information
  • Execute predefined workflows
People decide
  • Load acceptance
  • Pricing negotiations
  • Driver instructions
  • Unusual safety situations
  • Contractual disputes
  • Exceptions
  • Regulatory interpretation
Why the line is drawn there. An incorrect AI recommendation is an operational problem (Section 28.1). The mitigations are human approval, business rules, confidence thresholds, monitoring, audit trails and continuous evaluation — which only work if a person is still in the path.
Sections 6 & 28.2

Regulatory posture

FMCSA's Electronic Logging Device rules apply to most motor carriers and drivers required to maintain records of duty status. ELDs must meet specified technical requirements and be certified and registered as required by FMCSA, and carriers are responsible for using compliant devices.

DispatchIQ therefore does not represent itself as replacing an FMCSA-compliant ELD. The planned architecture integrates with existing compliant ELD and fleet-management systems, consumes authorized operational data from them, and uses it to assist dispatchers.

This posture comes out of the founder's own compliance research: her 2026 reference work on U.S. and EU dispatch compliance addresses ELD recordkeeping obligations, hours-of-service enforcement and broker liability exposure — the same issues that define where an AI dispatch product must defer to certified systems rather than substitute for them.

Nothing on this site is legal or regulatory advice. Verify your own obligations against current FMCSA guidance and a qualified professional.
